# Own Delivery App for Your Restaurant: Is It Worth Leaving DoorDash? Real Costs 2026

Building your own restaurant delivery app costs between **$25,000 and $80,000**, with monthly maintenance of **$1,500–$4,000**. If you do $80,000/month through DoorDash at a 28% commission, that's $22,400 leaving your account every month, so a custom app often pays for itself in 1 to 4 months. The smart move for most restaurants isn't leaving the marketplaces entirely, it's running both: platforms for acquisition, your own app for retention where the margin is 100% yours.

I'm Pedro Corgnati, Founder of SystemForge and a full-stack developer who has shipped custom ordering and delivery apps for SMBs, including restaurants that were bleeding five figures a month to marketplace fees. Almost none of those owners wanted to "kill DoorDash." They wanted to stop paying a third of their best customers' orders to a platform that already had those customers loyal to *them*. This article gives you the real numbers, the comparison math, and the honest answer on when an own app is worth it and when it isn't.

## How much does building your own restaurant delivery app cost — direct answer

Cost scales with features, not with how fancy the marketing deck looks. Here's how real projects break down in 2026.

| Tier | What you get | One-time build | Monthly maintenance |
|------|--------------|----------------|---------------------|
| Basic | Menu + ordering + single payment + order tracking | $25,000–$40,000 | $1,500–$2,200 |
| Full | Loyalty, push notifications, multi-payment, promos | $40,000–$60,000 | $2,200–$3,200 |
| Premium | Multi-location, BI analytics, POS integration, white-label | $60,000–$80,000 | $3,200–$4,000 |

Maintenance isn't optional padding. It covers app store renewals, OS updates (iOS and Android break things twice a year), payment gateway changes, security patches, and bug fixes. A delivery app that nobody maintains stops accepting payments within a year.

The range is wide because "delivery app" means very different things. A single-location taqueria needs far less than a five-location pizza chain with kitchen routing and a loyalty program. Get an honest scope before you anchor on a number.

**Get a no-obligation quote** for your specific menu and locations before you assume you're in the premium tier — most single-location restaurants land in Basic or Full.

## What DoorDash actually charges: the real commission math

DoorDash, Uber Eats, and Grubhub charge restaurants **15% to 30% per order**, depending on the plan you accept. The lower tiers exist, but they bury your restaurant in search results, so most owners end up on the 25–30% partnership plans to stay visible.

Run your own numbers. At $80,000/month in marketplace delivery revenue and a 28% blended commission, you're paying **$22,400 every month**, which is $268,800 a year. That's before the customer-facing fees the platform adds on top, which quietly suppress your order volume.

Here's the part owners miss: a meaningful slice of those orders are **repeat customers who already know your restaurant**. You're paying acquisition prices for retention traffic. That's the leak an own app is designed to plug.

There's a second cost most owners never put a number on: you don't own the customer relationship. DoorDash holds the email, the order history, and the marketing channel. When they run a promotion for a competitor down the street, your regulars see it inside the same app they used to order from you. You're effectively renting access to customers you already earned, and the rent goes up every time the platform "adjusts" its partnership tiers. An own app flips that, the data, the inbox, and the home-screen real estate become yours.

It won't send push notifications or live on the home screen, but it stops the commission bleed on customers who'd order direct anyway.

### Digital menu vs native app: which to choose

Start with a digital menu if your delivery revenue is under roughly $30,000/month or you want to validate that customers will order direct at all. Move to a native app once you're paying more in monthly commissions than the app's monthly maintenance, and you want push notifications and a home-screen icon working for you.

### Push notifications: the weapon the platforms don't give you

When a customer orders through DoorDash, DoorDash markets to them, not you. With your own app, a Friday 5 p.m. push ("Free garlic knots on orders over $30 tonight") goes straight to people who already love your food, at zero marginal cost. This single feature is why retention through an own channel beats marketplace economics over time.

### Payment gateway: credit cards, ACH, Apple Pay

A US restaurant app needs Stripe or Square handling cards, Apple Pay, and Google Pay, with ACH as an option for larger catering tickets. Processing runs about 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction. Compare that 3% to a 28% marketplace cut and the math speaks for itself.

## What a restaurant delivery app must have

Skip any of these and the app underperforms, no matter how much you spent on it.

- **Live order tracking** customers expect the DoorDash experience; anything worse feels broken.
- **Reliable kitchen routing** orders must hit the kitchen display or printer without manual re-entry.
- **POS integration** Toast, Square, or Clover, so inventory and reporting stay in one place.
- **Loyalty or rewards** the reason a customer opens your app instead of DoorDash.
- **Push notifications** your only free, direct marketing channel.
- **Clean checkout** Apple Pay and saved cards; every extra tap loses orders.

One that's easy to forget: **CCPA compliance** if you collect customer data and serve California. Your app needs a privacy policy, a data deletion path, and honest opt-ins. We build this in from day one, because retrofitting it after launch is painful.

## Real case: restaurant that reduced dependency on DoorDash (and what happened)

A regional pizza chain (three locations, anonymized) was doing about **$100,000/month** in delivery, almost all through DoorDash and Uber Eats at a blended ~28%. That's roughly $28,000/month in commissions.

We built them a Full-tier app: menu, loyalty points, push notifications, Stripe with Apple Pay, and Toast POS integration. The launch playbook mattered as much as the code: a QR code on every box and table offering 15% off the **first in-app order**, plus a loyalty point structure that only the app could redeem.

Within four months, about **40% of their delivery volume** had shifted to the own app. On that $40,000 of migrated revenue, they went from ~28% commission to ~3% processing, saving roughly **$11,200/month**, around $134,000 a year. They kept both marketplaces running for new-customer acquisition. The app paid back its build cost in well under a year.

Realistic note: that 40% migration didn't happen by magic. It took the QR incentive, the loyalty hook, and consistent push campaigns. Restaurants that build an app and do no promotion typically migrate under 10%.

Two operational details made the difference. First, the Toast integration meant in-app orders dropped straight onto the kitchen screen with no manual re-entry, so order accuracy held steady even as volume shifted channels. Second, they didn't try to convert everyone at once, they targeted their highest-frequency customers first with a personal push offer, then let word of mouth and the table QR codes do the slower work. By month six the migrated share had crept past 45%, and the commission savings funded a part-time marketing hire who kept the push calendar full. That's the compounding effect an own channel gives you that a marketplace never will.

## Mistakes to avoid when building a restaurant delivery app

1. **Building before you've done the commission math.** If your marketplace fees don't dwarf the app's monthly cost, an app is a vanity project. Run the numbers first.
2. **Treating launch as the finish line.** The app is 50% of the work; the QR incentive, loyalty hook, and push campaigns are the other 50%. Skip them and migration stalls under 10%.
3. **Killing DoorDash on day one.** The marketplaces still bring genuinely new customers. Keep them for acquisition, win retention on your own app.
4. **Cheaping out on POS integration.** Manual order re-entry causes kitchen errors and refunds that erase your commission savings. Integrate properly.
5. **Ignoring maintenance.** An unmaintained app breaks at the next iOS update and starts rejecting payments silently. Budget for it.

## When to hire vs do it in-house

Build an own app (with help) when **your monthly marketplace commissions exceed roughly $5,000**, you have repeat customers who'd order direct given a reason, and you can commit to running the launch promotion. The payback math is on your side.

Stay on a **digital menu or marketplaces only** when your delivery revenue is under ~$30,000/month, you have no in-house way to run loyalty and push campaigns, or your repeat-order rate is low. A $25,000 app you can't fill with orders is worse than a 28% commission.

The clean rule of thumb: when monthly commissions you'd realistically reclaim exceed monthly app maintenance, an own app moves from "expense" to "investment."

## FAQ — Frequently asked questions

### How much does a restaurant delivery app cost in 2026?
Between $25,000 and $80,000 to build, plus $1,500–$4,000/month maintenance. Basic apps (menu, ordering, payment) start around $25,000; premium multi-location apps with BI reach $80,000. Your tier depends on locations, loyalty, and POS integration needs.

### How long does it take to build?
Typically 8 to 16 weeks from kickoff to App Store and Google Play, depending on tier and POS integration. Basic apps land near the shorter end; multi-location premium builds with deep Toast or Square integration take longer.

### Do I have to leave DoorDash?
No, and you usually shouldn't. Marketplaces are good at bringing genuinely new customers. Keep them for acquisition and use your own app for retention, where margins are 100% yours instead of 70%.

### How do I get customers to actually use my app?
Give them a reason only the app offers: an exclusive first-order discount via QR codes on tables and bags, plus a loyalty program that only redeems in-app. Then use push notifications to bring regulars back. Adoption is a promotion problem, not a tech one.

### Does it work for a small restaurant?
If your delivery revenue is under ~$30,000/month, start with a low-cost digital menu instead of a native app. Move to an app once your monthly marketplace commissions clearly exceed an app's monthly maintenance cost.
