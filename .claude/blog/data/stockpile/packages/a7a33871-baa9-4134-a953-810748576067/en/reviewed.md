# How Much Does a Business Website Cost in 2026? Real Ranges for US Companies

*By Pedro Corgnati, Founder of SystemForge*

A professional business website in the US costs between **$3,000 and $50,000** in 2026, depending on scope, features, and who builds it. A simple 5–8 page informational site with contact forms runs $3,000–$8,000. A service business site with booking, client portals, or CRM integration usually lands at $8,000–$20,000. E-commerce starts around $10,000 and scales past $50,000 for custom platforms. Site builders like Wix or Squarespace cost $200–$500 a year but hit hard limits as you grow.

I've spent the last several years building custom software and websites for small and mid-sized businesses, and the single most common question I get on a first call is some version of "what should this actually cost me?" Most articles answer with a shrug and an "it depends." You deserve better than that, so this guide gives you real 2026 US ranges tied to real scope, explains why two quotes for the "same" site can differ by 10x, and helps you decide whether you need custom work or a template.

## The short answer: website cost by type (2026 US pricing)

The fastest way to find your number is to match your business to a website type. Each tier below assumes professional work, not a DIY weekend project, and the timeline assumes you deliver your content (text and images) on schedule.

| Website type | Typical 2026 US cost | Timeline | What's included |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple informational (5–8 pages) | $3,000–$8,000 | 4–8 weeks | Custom design, mobile responsive, contact form, basic SEO |
| Service business with booking | $8,000–$20,000 | 8–16 weeks | Scheduling, client portal, CRM connection, payments |
| E-commerce | $10,000–$50,000 | 10–20 weeks | Product catalog, cart, payment gateway, inventory |
| Custom web app or portal | $25,000–$100,000+ | 3–9 months | User accounts, dashboards, backend, integrations |

### Simple informational website (5–8 pages)

This is the home, about, services, contact setup most local businesses need. At $3,000–$8,000 you get a custom design (not a stock theme everyone else uses), proper mobile layout, a working contact form that emails you reliably, and on-page SEO so Google can actually index you. Below $3,000 from a professional almost always means a template with light edits.

### Service business website with booking or contact flow

Once your site has to *do* something beyond informing, the cost moves up. Booking integrations, a client login area, quote requests that feed a CRM, and payment collection each add engineering work. A clinic, a law office, or a home-services company usually lands in the $8,000–$20,000 band because the flows have to be reliable, not just pretty.

### E-commerce website

Selling online means a catalog, a cart, a payment gateway, tax and shipping logic, and inventory that stays in sync. A focused store on a managed platform can start near $10,000. Custom storefronts with bespoke checkout, subscriptions, or B2B pricing climb toward $50,000 and beyond. The cost lives in the edge cases: refunds, failed payments, out-of-stock states.

### Custom web application or portal

When users log in to *do work* (track orders, manage accounts, book and reschedule, see dashboards) you're building a web app, not a website. That means a backend, a database, authentication, and security review. These start at $25,000 and routinely pass $100,000 for anything with real business logic. If a vendor quotes you $5,000 for "a portal," read the fine print.

## What actually drives website cost

Two quotes for what looks like the same website can differ wildly because they're priced on different variables. Here's where the money actually goes.

### Design complexity: custom vs. template

A template starts at a finished design and bends your content to fit it. Custom design starts at your brand and builds the layout around how your customers actually decide to buy. Template setups are cheaper and faster; custom design costs more because someone is making hundreds of deliberate choices about hierarchy, trust, and conversion. For a brochure site, a good template is fine. For a site that has to out-convert competitors, custom usually pays for itself.

### Number of pages and content

Each unique page template is design plus build plus responsive testing. Eight pages built from three templates is cheap. Forty pages with twelve distinct layouts is not. And content is the silent budget item: if you don't have copy and photography ready, you're either paying the agency to produce it or delaying the whole project. Content delays are the number one reason websites ship late.

### Integrations (CRM, booking, payments, email marketing)

Every connection to an outside system is engineering and testing. Hooking up a calendar, a CRM, a payment processor, or an email platform each adds hours, and the testing matters more than the wiring. A booking flow that double-books or a payment form that silently fails costs you customers, so this is the wrong place to cut corners.

### Ongoing maintenance and hosting

A website is not a one-time purchase. Plan for hosting, security updates, dependency patches, and small content changes. A site left untouched for two years becomes a security liability and a slow, dated embarrassment. Budget for ownership, not just the launch.

### SEO and performance optimization

Ranking and speed aren't automatic. Real SEO means proper site structure, fast load times, clean markup, and content built around what people search. A site that loads in four seconds loses visitors and rankings to one that loads in one. Performance work is part of why a $15,000 site outperforms a $3,000 one long after launch.

## Website builders vs. custom development: which is right for your business?

This is the real fork in the road, and the honest answer is that builders are the right call for some businesses and a trap for others.

### When Wix, Squarespace, or Webflow is the right choice

If you need a clean online presence, you have low traffic, and your needs are mostly informational, a builder is genuinely a smart, cheap start. Squarespace Business runs about $23/month, Wix Business around $27/month, and Webflow Business around $36/month. For a new solo business validating an idea, spending $300 a year instead of $8,000 is the correct decision. I'll tell clients this directly when it's true.

### When you need a custom developer

You've outgrown the builder when you need specific integrations the platform won't do cleanly, when page speed is hurting your Google rankings, when your team is fighting the editor to make routine changes, or when your site has to handle real business logic. At that point the builder's monthly fee is the small cost; the lost customers and capped growth are the big one.

### The hidden costs of website builders for growing businesses

Builders look cheap until you add the premium plan, the third-party booking app, the e-commerce add-on, transaction fees, and a freelancer to make it look less generic. Stack those and a "$300/year" site quietly becomes $2,000–$4,000/year, with a ceiling you'll hit anyway. The platform owns your data and your design system, and migrating off later is its own project. Cheap upfront is not the same as cheap over three years.

## Who builds your website, and why it matters

The same scope costs different amounts depending on who's holding the keyboard. Understanding the tiers helps you read a quote.

### Freelancers ($1,500–$8,000 range)

A good freelancer is cost-effective for a focused, well-defined site, typically billing $50–$150/hour in the US. The risk is bandwidth and continuity: one person gets sick, takes another client, or disappears. For a simple site with clear scope, a vetted freelancer is often the best value. For anything mission-critical, the single-point-of-failure risk is real.

### Boutique agencies ($5,000–$25,000 range)

A small studio gives you a team (designer, developer, project lead) without big-agency overhead. You get process, continuity, and someone who answers when something breaks. This is the sweet spot for most established SMBs that need the site to work and to keep working. Rates usually run $100–$250/hour.

### Large agencies ($25,000–$100,000+)

Big agencies bring deep teams, brand strategy, and the ability to handle complex builds, with overhead and account layers priced in. For an enterprise or a heavily regulated brand, that structure earns its cost. For a 12-person local business, you're often paying for a process built for companies 50 times your size.

### Nearshore development firms: quality at better rates

Nearshore teams (working in or near US time zones) blend agency-grade process with rates around $30–$80/hour. You get overlapping hours, strong English, and senior engineering without the largest-agency markup. This is the model I built SystemForge around, because most US SMBs want agency quality at a price that respects their budget.

## Ongoing costs after your website launches

Launch is the beginning of ownership, not the end of spending. Here's what a realistic annual budget looks like.

| Recurring item | Typical 2026 US cost |
|---|---|
| Hosting | $10–$500/month (by traffic and platform) |
| Domain + SSL | $15–$100/year (SSL often bundled) |
| Maintenance & updates | $500–$2,000+/year |
| SEO & content | $500–$3,000/month (optional, growth-driven) |

A fully loaded ownership budget for a US SMB site typically runs **$5,000–$15,000/year** once you include hosting, maintenance, and any active marketing. The average small business spends $1,200–$3,600/year on maintenance alone. Plan for it upfront so it isn't a surprise.

## Website cost by industry vertical

Different industries need different flows, which shifts the typical range. These are starting benchmarks; your scope sets the final number.

- **Restaurant:** $4,000–$12,000. Menu, hours, location, reservations or online ordering. Ordering platforms push toward the web-app tier.
- **Law firm:** $6,000–$20,000. Practice-area pages, attorney bios, trust signals, intake forms, and often case-management or consult-booking integrations.
- **Medical or dental clinic:** $8,000–$25,000. Appointment booking, patient intake, and privacy-conscious handling of any sensitive data.
- **Real estate agency:** $8,000–$30,000. Listing feeds (IDX/MLS), property search, lead capture, and map-driven browsing.
- **Startup:** $5,000–$20,000. Often a sharp marketing site plus a path to the product or waitlist; scales with funding stage.
- **Retail / e-commerce:** $10,000–$50,000+. Catalog, checkout, inventory, and the operational glue behind orders.

## How to get an accurate website quote

A quote is only as good as the brief behind it. A little prep saves you money and prevents the "that wasn't included" conversation later.

### What to prepare before talking to a developer

Come with your goal (more leads, online sales, fewer phone calls), a rough page list, examples of sites you like and why, the integrations you need, whether you have content ready, and a realistic budget band. The more concrete you are, the more accurate and comparable the quotes you get back.

### Red flags in website proposals

Be cautious of a flat price with no scope behind it, no mention of mobile or performance, no maintenance plan, vague "SEO included" with no specifics, and anyone who promises a custom professional site for a few hundred dollars. A quote that's dramatically lower than the others usually means a smaller scope, not a better deal.

### Questions to ask before signing

Ask who owns the code and the domain, what happens if you part ways, how revisions and change requests are handled, what's included in support after launch, and how they handle performance and SEO. Clear answers signal a professional; hedging signals future friction.

## Most common mistakes US businesses make

- **Buying on price alone.** The cheapest quote almost always means the smallest scope. You feel the gap the day a form breaks or you can't update your hours.
- **Skipping the content plan.** Half-finished copy and stock photos sink an otherwise good build. Content is the project, not an afterthought.
- **Ignoring mobile.** Most local traffic is on phones. A desktop-first site quietly loses the majority of its visitors.
- **Forgetting ongoing costs.** A site with no maintenance budget becomes slow, dated, and insecure within a couple of years.
- **Over-building too early.** A brand-new business rarely needs a $40,000 custom app. Start right-sized and grow into complexity.

## When to hire a pro vs. do it in-house

Use measurable criteria, not gut feel.

**Do it yourself (builder) when:** you're pre-revenue or validating, you need fewer than ~8 pages, you have no complex integrations, your monthly traffic is in the low hundreds, and the site is mostly informational. The math favors $300/year over $8,000.

**Hire a professional when:** the site drives real revenue, you need integrations (booking, CRM, payments), page speed or SEO is costing you rankings, your team wastes hours fighting the editor, or you handle sensitive data. If the site failing for a day would cost you measurable money, that's your signal. As a rule of thumb, when the projected annual value of the website clearly exceeds the build cost, hiring is the rational choice.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much should I budget for a small business website in 2026?**
For a real professional site, budget at least $3,000–$5,000 for a simple build and $8,000–$15,000 if you need booking, integrations, or anything beyond basic pages. Significantly cheaper usually means a template with minimal customization and weak mobile optimization.

**Is it worth paying for a custom website instead of using Wix or Squarespace?**
It depends on your growth stage. Builders are fine for a basic presence with low traffic. The hidden costs appear when you need real integrations, want to rank in Google, or outgrow template limits. Most growing businesses hit those walls within two to three years.

**Why do some agencies quote $3,000 and others $30,000 for the "same" website?**
The drivers are who builds it, how much is custom design and code, whether strategy and SEO are included, and how much post-launch support comes with it. A $3,000 quote often means a light template; a $30,000 quote typically includes strategy, custom UX, performance work, and support.

**How long does it take to build a business website?**
A simple 5-page site takes 4–6 weeks. A service site with integrations takes 8–12 weeks. E-commerce takes 10–20 weeks. These assume you deliver content on time. Content delays are the most common reason projects ship late.

**What ongoing costs should I expect after launch?**
Plan for hosting ($20–$200/month), domain renewal ($15–$50/year), SSL (often bundled), and maintenance ($100–$300/month or a yearly plan). Add $500–$3,000/month if you run SEO or content. A loaded ownership budget for a US SMB is roughly $5,000–$15,000/year.

**Can I get a professional website for under $1,000?**
Not custom work from a professional. Under $1,000 means a DIY template, a very basic freelancer setup, or offshore work of unknown quality. For most US businesses trying to win customers online, a $1,000 site tends to look like one and competes accordingly.

**What's the difference between a website and a web app?**
A website informs and captures leads; it's mostly static. A web app lets users log in and do things: track orders, book, manage accounts. A restaurant menu page is a website; an online ordering platform is a web app. Web apps cost more because they need a backend, a database, and security work.
