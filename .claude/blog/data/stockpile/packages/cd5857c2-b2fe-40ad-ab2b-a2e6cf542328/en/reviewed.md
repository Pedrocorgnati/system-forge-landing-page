# Custom Restaurant Software in 2026: Ordering Apps, POS, Loyalty, and Delivery Platforms

Custom restaurant software in 2026 costs between **$15,000 and $120,000+** depending on scope. A direct online ordering system that kills third-party commissions runs $15,000–$35,000; a custom loyalty platform adds $10,000–$25,000; a full app with ordering, loyalty, and delivery dispatch reaches $60,000–$120,000. These are one-time build costs you weigh against ongoing DoorDash commissions (15–30%) or Toast fees ($110–$800/month per location). For operators with steady delivery volume, the build usually pays for itself in 12–24 months.

The reason is simple: every dollar a restaurant pays in delivery commission is a dollar that vanishes monthly with nothing to show for it. A direct ordering channel converts that bleed into an asset you own. This guide gives you the real numbers and a framework to decide where custom makes sense and where it doesn't.

## Why US restaurant operators are building custom software in 2026

Off-premise revenue (delivery plus takeout) now accounts for roughly 60% of US restaurant sales. That shift handed a lot of leverage to the platforms sitting between you and your customer, and operators have started doing the math on what that middleman actually costs.

### The commission problem: DoorDash, Grubhub, and Uber Eats

The headline rates in 2026 are brutal: DoorDash takes 15–25%, Grubhub 15–30%, and Uber Eats 15–30% per order. On a $25 order, that's $4–$8 gone before you cover food, labor, or rent.

Run it at scale and it gets worse. A restaurant doing $50,000/month in delivery at a 25% commission hands $12,500/month to the platforms. That's $150,000 a year leaving the building, and you never see the customer's email, phone, or order history. You're renting access to your own diners.

### Data ownership: the hidden value of your own ordering system

When an order comes through DoorDash, the customer belongs to DoorDash. You can't email them a Tuesday promo, you can't see that they order the same wings every Friday, and you can't bring them back when they drift.

A direct ordering system flips that. Every order builds your list, your loyalty data, and your ability to market without paying a toll each time. Over a couple of years, that owned customer base is often worth more than the commission you saved.

### When off-the-shelf POS isn't enough

Toast, Square, and Lightspeed are good products. For a single location running standard service, they're frequently the right call, and I'll tell a client that to their face.

The limits show up at the edges: multi-location reporting that doesn't match how you actually run, loyalty rules the platform won't support, a branded app you can't have, or an integration between your POS and a kitchen or inventory system that simply doesn't exist. That's where custom earns its keep, usually alongside your POS rather than replacing it.

### Direct online ordering system ($15k–$35k)

This is the highest-ROI starting point for most operators. A mobile-optimized web ordering flow with menu management, Stripe payments, tip handling, and order notifications to your kitchen. No commission per order, no app store, fastest path to keeping the margin you're currently giving away.

### Mobile ordering app, iOS and Android ($30k–$60k)

A native app adds push notifications, saved payment methods, Apple/Google Pay, and the home-screen presence that drives repeat orders. It costs 50–100% more than web and brings app store review and ongoing maintenance. Worth it once online order volume is consistent, not before.

### Loyalty and rewards platform ($10k–$25k)

Points, digital punch cards, and rewards rules wired directly into your ordering flow. Compared to third-party loyalty apps at $50–$200/month, a one-time build pays back in roughly 5–15 months and keeps the customer data under your roof.

### POS integration ($15k–$30k)

Connecting custom ordering, loyalty, or reporting to Square, Toast, or Clover via their APIs. This is what lets you keep the POS your staff already knows while adding the capabilities it lacks.

### Delivery dispatch and driver management ($20k–$40k)

For operators running their own drivers or a hybrid model: route assignment, live tracking, driver app, and dispatch logic. Makes sense when in-house delivery volume justifies the coordination overhead.

## ROI calculation: custom software vs. third-party platforms

The build-vs-buy decision isn't a vibe. It's a payback period you can calculate before you spend a dollar.

### Commission cost analysis by revenue tier

Take your monthly delivery revenue and multiply by your blended commission rate (most operators land around 25%). That's your monthly platform bill.

- $20,000/month in delivery → ~$5,000/month in commissions
- $50,000/month in delivery → ~$12,500/month in commissions
- $100,000/month in delivery → ~$25,000/month in commissions

### Breakeven timeline

A $35,000 custom ordering system against a $12,500/month commission bill breaks even in under three months on paper. Reality is softer: you won't move 100% of delivery off the platforms overnight, and you'll keep some marketplace presence for discovery.

Plan on shifting 30–50% of delivery volume to your direct channel in the first year. Even at that conservative pace, most operators doing $30,000+ in monthly delivery clear breakeven inside 12–24 months, then bank the savings indefinitely.

### Customer data: what you own vs. what you lease

The commission savings are the headline. The owned customer list is the compounding return. An email list of 5,000 past customers you can market to for free is an asset that grows in value; a DoorDash placement is a cost that recurs forever.

We started small on purpose: a web ordering system with menu sync to their existing Square setup, Stripe payments with tip handling, and a simple loyalty layer (points plus a "reorder my usual" button). The first phase came in around $28,000 and launched in ten weeks. No native app yet, no driver dispatch, just the channel they were missing.

On paper, the build pays back in well under a year, and the owned list keeps compounding past that.

What made it work wasn't the tech, it was sequencing. We didn't try to kill the marketplaces; we captured the loyal core and left discovery to the platforms. Phase two (a native app) is now on the table because the volume finally justifies it, not because someone sold them an app on day one.

## Key features that actually get used

I've watched the analytics on these apps, so build for what customers touch, not what demos well.

High usage: one-tap reorder ("get my usual"), loyalty balance and reward redemption, live order status, and digital receipts. These drive repeat orders and should be in phase one.

Lower usage than operators expect: in-app table reservations (OpenTable and Resy already own that habit), split-bill math, and social sharing. They sound impressive in a kickoff meeting and then sit unused. Spend the budget where the taps are.

## Technical architecture, briefly

Restaurant software lives or dies on real-time order management: an order placed on the customer's phone has to hit the kitchen reliably in seconds, with clear status back to the diner. That's the part cheap builds get wrong.

POS integration runs through Square, Toast, or Clover APIs for menu sync and order push. For a hybrid model, you can still pull orders from delivery marketplaces into one queue so your staff isn't juggling four tablets. And for most operators the answer to "app or website?" is *both, in sequence*: web first, native app once volume justifies it.

One detail that quietly decides a project: how the system handles menu availability in real time. If you 86 a dish on the line, the ordering system has to reflect it instantly, or you're refunding orders and apologizing all night. Good builds wire availability straight into the POS or the kitchen display so the menu the customer sees is always the menu you can actually cook. It's unglamorous plumbing, and it's exactly the kind of thing off-the-shelf platforms handle inconsistently across integrations.

## Most common mistakes restaurant operators make

**1. Building the app before the web ordering system.** The app costs more, ships slower, and most of your first direct orders will come from a link you text or post, not the App Store. Web first, always.

**2. Replacing a POS that works.** A full POS swap is disruptive and rarely necessary. Integrate with what your staff already knows unless you have a hard requirement it genuinely can't meet.

**3. Skipping the loyalty hook at launch.** A reorder button and a points balance are what turn a one-time delivery into a regular. Bolting them on later means re-engaging customers you already lost.

**4. Ignoring kitchen workflow.** Software that doesn't match how your line actually fires orders creates chaos at the pass. The build has to fit the kitchen, not the other way around.

**5. Chasing 100% off-platform too fast.** Marketplaces still drive discovery. The smart play is a direct channel that captures your repeat customers while you keep a lean marketplace presence for new ones.

## When to hire out vs. do it in-house

Build custom when the math clears: you're doing **$30,000+/month in delivery**, paying **$5,000+/month in commissions**, and you have at least 6–12 months of runway to recoup the build. Multi-location operators and anyone whose loyalty or reporting needs exceed their POS are strong candidates.

Stay off-the-shelf when you're early-stage, doing **under ~$10,000/month** in delivery, or running a single location with standard service. At that volume Square or Toast plus a basic ordering integration is the right, honest answer, and I'll tell you so rather than sell you a build you don't need yet.

The clean test: if your annual commission spend is larger than the one-time build cost, custom almost always wins. If it isn't, wait and revisit when your volume crosses that line.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it worth building a custom ordering app instead of using DoorDash?**
For restaurants with consistent delivery volume, usually yes, within 12–24 months. If you're paying $5,000–$15,000/month in commissions, a $25k–$35k ordering system has a clear payback. The bonus is owning your customer data instead of leasing it.

**Can I keep using Toast or Square and still add custom features?**
Yes. Most projects integrate *with* your existing POS rather than replacing it. Toast and Square offer developer APIs that let custom ordering, loyalty, and reporting tools connect to your current setup. Full POS replacement is a bigger investment, usually only worth it for chains with specific needs.

**How long does it take to build a restaurant ordering app?**
A web-based ordering system: 8–12 weeks. A native iOS and Android app with loyalty: 16–24 weeks. A full platform with POS integration, dispatch, and analytics: 6–9 months. Most operators launch web first and add the mobile app in phase two.

**Do I need an app or is a website enough?**
A mobile-optimized website with integrated ordering is enough for most restaurants starting out. Native apps add push notifications and Apple/Google Pay but cost 50–100% more and require app store management. Rule of thumb: start with web, add the app at $3,000+ in monthly online orders.

**How does custom loyalty software compare to apps like Stamp Me or Belly?**
Third-party loyalty apps cost $50–$200/month but lock in your customer data and stamp their brand on your experience. Custom loyalty is a one-time $10k–$25k build with full data ownership and native integration into your ordering flow. Breakeven is typically 5–15 months.

**What's the cheapest way to start with custom restaurant software?**
A direct web ordering system at $15k–$35k. It eliminates per-order commissions immediately, requires no app store, and ships in 8–12 weeks. Prove the channel, then reinvest the saved commissions into the native app and loyalty features.
