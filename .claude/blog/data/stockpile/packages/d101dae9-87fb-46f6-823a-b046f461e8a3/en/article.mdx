# Custom Software for Construction Companies: Cost, Field Apps, and Dispatch (2026)

Custom software for a construction company in the US costs between **$20,000 and $120,000** depending on scope. A job scheduling and dispatch system runs $20,000–$40,000, a field service app with crew tracking and daily reports lands at $25,000–$50,000, and a full project management platform with estimating and job costing reaches $60,000–$120,000. The upfront number is higher than a monthly SaaS subscription, but for a mid-size contractor it usually costs less over three years and fits the way you actually work.

I'm Pedro Corgnati, Founder of SystemForge. Across the custom builds we've delivered for SMBs, the construction projects are the ones where the gap between "the software the industry sells" and "the workflow the business runs" is widest. A 15-person framing contractor does not work like a 2,000-person general contractor, but Procore prices and structures the product as if they do. This guide gives you the honest build-vs-buy math, real cost ranges, and the order I'd build things in.

## Why US construction companies are building custom software

Construction is one of the least digitized industries in the country. Roughly 60% of contractors still run some part of job management on paper or spreadsheets, according to JBKnowledge's Construction Technology Report. That is not because owners are behind the times. It is because the tools on the market are either too expensive, too generic, or both.

### The Procore problem: enterprise pricing for SMB workflows

Procore is excellent software built for large general contractors. The pricing reflects that. At roughly $375–$500 per user per month, a 20-person company can pay $90,000–$120,000 a year before anyone has logged a single daily report.

The painful part is what most SMBs actually use. I've watched 10-person specialty contractors pay for a platform where they touch maybe 30% of the features. They're funding an enterprise feature set so they can use a scheduling calendar and a photo log.

### When spreadsheets stop working

Spreadsheets are not a failure. They're a perfectly rational starting point, right up until they aren't. The warning signs are consistent across the contractors I talk to.

You know spreadsheets have broken when the dispatch board lives in one person's head, when two crews show up to the same job because nobody updated the master sheet, when job costs are reconstructed at month-end instead of tracked live, and when a foreman texts photos that nobody can find two weeks later. At that point the spreadsheet is not saving money. It is hiding the cost of rework, double-bookings, and slow billing.

### What off-the-shelf tools can't do for specialty contractors

General platforms are designed around a general contractor's mental model: projects, submittals, RFIs, drawings. A concrete sub, an HVAC outfit, or a fire-protection contractor has a different reality, where the scheduling unit is a crew and a piece of equipment, not a drawing set.

When your real workflow doesn't map onto the software's assumptions, your team builds workarounds in spreadsheets next to the expensive tool. That is the moment custom starts to make financial sense.

### Job scheduling and dispatch system ($20k–$40k)

This is the operational core: who goes where, when, with what crew and what materials. A good dispatch system gives the office a drag-and-drop board and gives crews a mobile view of their day. It is the single highest-leverage thing most contractors can build.

### Field service app with crew tracking ($25k–$50k)

Daily logs, time tracking, photo documentation, punch lists, and GPS check-in, all built offline-first so they work where cell service doesn't. This is where field and office finally share one source of truth.

### Estimating and bidding software ($20k–$35k)

If your estimating logic is your competitive edge, a generic calculator won't capture it. Custom estimating encodes your assemblies, your labor rates, and your markup rules, and pushes accepted bids straight into the project.

### Project management portal ($30k–$60k)

Budgets, change orders, subcontractor documents, and client-facing dashboards in one place. This is usually phase two or three, after dispatch and field reporting are paying for themselves.

### Full-featured construction platform ($60k–$120k)

Everything connected: estimate to schedule to field to job cost to invoice, with QuickBooks sync underneath. Built in phases, never big-bang. Anyone selling you a single 12-month monolith is selling you risk.

## Key features construction companies actually need

Not every build needs everything. The features below are the ones that earn their keep, roughly in the order most contractors should add them.

**Job scheduling and crew assignment.** A live board where dispatch changes reach the crew's phone instantly, and where the office can see who is committed where.

**Field reporting: daily logs, photos, punch lists.** Structured daily reports with timestamped, geotagged photos beat a flood of unsortable text messages. Punch lists that close out on-device keep jobs moving.

**Material tracking and purchase orders.** Knowing what's ordered, delivered, and installed prevents the two most expensive site delays: missing material and double-ordered material.

**Job costing and budget vs. actual.** Live job cost is the difference between finding out you lost money on month-end and catching it while you can still fix it. This is the feature owners value most once they have it.

**Subcontractor portals and document sharing.** A controlled space for subs to pull current drawings, submit invoices, and confirm schedule, without endless email chains.

**Client-facing project dashboards.** A simple status view for the owner of the project reduces "where are we?" calls and quietly becomes a sales asset for the next bid.

## Mobile first: field apps for construction crews

About 65% of construction workers access job information on mobile, and the job site is the worst connectivity environment in your business. A field app that assumes a live connection is a field app that fails the moment a crew drives into a basement or a rural site.

### What a field app needs to work on job sites

Offline-first architecture is non-negotiable. The app caches data locally and syncs when connectivity returns, so daily reports, photos, time entries, and checklist completions all work with no signal. If a developer can't explain their offline conflict-resolution strategy, they haven't built for construction before.

### iOS vs. Android for construction crews

Most construction crews run a mix, and many companies issue rugged Android devices because they survive the site and cost less to replace. A cross-platform build (one codebase, both stores) usually serves field teams best and keeps long-term maintenance affordable. Reserve fully native builds for cases that genuinely need them.

### Integration with GPS and photo documentation

GPS check-in confirms who was on which job and when, and geotagged photos turn documentation into a defensible record for disputes and warranty claims. One note on tracking: GPS monitoring of employees during work hours is generally legal in the US, but disclosure requirements vary by state and California is stricter. A good build includes the disclosure and consent mechanisms from the start, not bolted on later.

## Integration requirements for construction software

Software that doesn't talk to your accounting system won't get used. Adoption dies the day your bookkeeper has to re-key everything.

### QuickBooks integration

About 72% of US construction companies run QuickBooks. It exposes a solid API (QuickBooks Online API, and the Desktop SDK for on-premise) that lets a custom system sync job costs, invoices, purchase orders, and vendor bills both directions. Your custom platform can push job-cost actuals into QuickBooks job costing, generate invoices from billing milestones, and pull vendor bills back in. Budget $5,000–$15,000 for this; it is almost always worth it.

### Sage 100 Contractor for larger GCs

Larger general contractors often run Sage 100 Contractor or Sage 300 CRE. These integrate too, though the work is heavier than QuickBooks. If Sage is your system of record, flag it during scoping because it changes the integration estimate.

### Procore API: connecting custom tools if you're already there

You don't always have to rip out Procore. If you're committed to it but missing a specific workflow, the Procore API lets a custom tool fill the gap, for example a specialized dispatch board or an estimating module that writes back into Procore. This is often the lowest-risk path for a company that's already invested.

## The most common mistakes contractors make

**Building the full platform first.** The single most expensive mistake. Companies try to replace everything at once, the project balloons, and they lose faith before anything ships. Build the dispatch slice, prove it, then expand.

**Skipping offline support to save money.** A field app that needs signal will get abandoned within a month on real job sites. Cutting offline to trim the quote guarantees you pay twice.

**Ignoring QuickBooks integration until the end.** Bolting accounting sync on after launch is harder and more expensive than scoping it in. Worse, without it adoption stalls because the office still re-keys everything.

**Hiring a developer with no construction context.** General app developers underestimate construction workflows every time, because the complexity is hidden until you're deep in change orders and retainage. Ask for relevant experience.

**Treating crews as an afterthought.** If the people in the field hate the app, the data never gets entered and the whole system is worthless. Crew usability is the project, not a detail.

## When to build custom vs. stay off-the-shelf

Use measurable criteria, not gut feel.

**Build custom when:** you have 5+ active projects and dispatch lives in someone's head or a fragile spreadsheet; you're paying for a tool where your team uses under half the features; your per-user SaaS cost exceeds roughly $4,000/month and keeps climbing with headcount; your estimating or billing logic is genuinely different from what templates allow; or your crews are abandoning the official tool for spreadsheets and group texts.

**Stay off-the-shelf when:** you're under ~5 people and a simple tool like Jobber ($29–$99/month) covers you; your workflow genuinely matches Buildertrend's residential model; you can't yet commit a stakeholder to a 10–16 week first phase; or you're growing so fast that your processes will change more than the software would. There's no shame in buying. The mistake is paying enterprise prices for an SMB workflow.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is custom construction software worth it compared to Procore or Buildertrend?**
It depends on fit. If you use most of Procore or Buildertrend and your workflow matches theirs, off-the-shelf can be cost-effective. Custom wins when your workflow is too specific, you're paying for unused features, or per-user pricing doesn't scale for your size. A 10-person sub paying $4,500/month for 30% of Procore is a clear custom candidate.

**What's the most important feature to build first?**
Job scheduling and crew dispatch. Deciding who goes where, when, and with what materials is where contractors feel the most pain with generic tools. Get this right, with mobile access for crews and visibility for the office, and you deliver immediate value. Estimation, billing, and client portals can follow in later phases.

**Can a custom field service app work without internet on job sites?**
Yes, if it's built offline-first. The app caches data locally and syncs when connectivity returns, so daily reports, photos, time tracking, and checklists all work with no signal. This is non-negotiable for construction. Confirm your developer has real offline-first experience before committing.

**How does custom construction software integrate with QuickBooks?**
Through the QuickBooks Online API or the Desktop SDK. A custom system can sync job costs, invoices, purchase orders, and vendor bills both directions, pushing job-cost actuals into QuickBooks job costing and creating invoices from billing milestones. Integration typically adds $5,000–$15,000 and is almost always worth it for adoption.

**How long does it take to build a custom construction system?**
A focused scheduling and dispatch system takes 10–16 weeks. A field service app with reporting runs 12–20 weeks. A full project management platform takes 6–12 months, built in phases. Timelines depend on workflow complexity, number of integrations, and how fast your team gives feedback during the build.

**Is it legal to track my crews with GPS?**
Generally yes during work hours, but requirements vary by state. Most states require you to disclose that company vehicles or devices may be tracked while working, and California is stricter. A well-built system includes the disclosure and consent mechanisms from the start. Your developer should flag this during scoping.
