When someone says "let's put a chatbot on our website," the question that should immediately follow is: "what kind of chatbot?" The answer radically changes the cost, maintenance complexity, and quality of user experience. There are inexpensive chatbots and chatbots that cost thousands of dollars per month — and both can be the right choice depending on the context.

There are three main architectures today: rule-based, NLP-based (natural language processing), and LLM-based (large language models like GPT-4 and Claude). Each solves different problems, has different costs, and requires different levels of technical maturity to operate. This article demystifies all three.

## Rule-Based Chatbots: Simple and Predictable

The rule-based chatbot is the oldest and still the most widely used. It works with a fixed set of flows: if the user types X, respond Y. If they type Z, respond W. The logic is a decision tree — menus, buttons, predefined responses.

Tools like Typebot, Landbot, and ManyChat are the visual version of this approach. You drag blocks, connect conditions, and publish. In a matter of hours you have a working bot that collects leads, qualifies customers, or answers the ten most common questions.

The advantages are clear: very low cost (many tools have free plans), 100% predictable behavior, easy to audit and fix, no risk of "hallucinations," and simple GDPR compliance because the bot never generates its own content.

The limitations are also clear: the bot doesn't understand language variations. If the flow expects "schedule a meeting" and the user types "I'd like to book a time," the bot doesn't know what to do. Any deviation from the expected script results in a generic fallback response.

When to use: static FAQ, lead qualification with predefined questions, scheduling with fixed options, structured data collection (name, email, company). Any flow where the possible responses are known and finite.

## NLP with Rasa and Dialogflow: Intents and Entities

The jump to NLP solves the language variation problem. Instead of mapping exact phrases, you train the model to recognize *intents* (what the user wants) and *entities* (the relevant data in the message).

"I want to schedule for Tuesday at 2pm" → intent: `schedule_appointment`, entity: `date=Tuesday`, entity: `time=2pm`

"Can I get an appointment next week in the morning" → same intent: `schedule_appointment`, entity: `date=next week`, entity: `time=morning`

**Dialogflow** (Google) is the easiest to get started with. Visual interface for creating intents, native support for multiple languages, direct integration with Google Assistant, Telegram, Slack, and WhatsApp. The CX (Customer Experience) version has more complex conversation flows and is recommended for serious projects. Cost is volume-based — accessible for medium volumes.

**Rasa** is the open-source alternative. You host it on your own server, train your own models, have full data control. It's more complex to configure (requires Python knowledge and ML concepts), but operational cost is much lower at high volume and data privacy is guaranteed — nothing leaves for third-party servers.

The weak point of classic NLP is that it still fails in open conversations and unexpected contexts. It recognizes trained intents, but if the user asks something outside the intent set, the bot doesn't know how to respond. Maintenance is constant: you need to review logs, identify recognition failures, and regularly add training examples.

## LLMs: High Quality, High Cost, Low Predictability

Large language models changed what's possible in a chatbot. With GPT-4, Claude, or Gemini, you have a bot that:

- Understands questions in any form
- Maintains context across long conversations
- Responds with fluent natural language
- Can reason over documents, FAQs, and knowledge bases

The most common architecture for support chatbots with LLMs is RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): you index your documentation, FAQs, and policies in a vector database (Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector). When the user asks something, the system retrieves the most relevant passages and passes them to the LLM along with the question. The model answers based on that context.

```python
from openai import OpenAI
from typing import Optional

client = OpenAI()

SYSTEM_PROMPT = """You are the virtual assistant for Company X.
Answer only based on the information provided in the context.
If you don't know the answer, say you'll connect with a human agent.
Always be polite and concise. Respond in English."""

def respond_to_user(
    question: str,
    retrieved_context: str,
    history: list[dict]
) -> str:
    messages = [
        {"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM_PROMPT},
        *history,
        {
            "role": "user",
            "content": f"Relevant context:\n{retrieved_context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"
        }
    ]

    response = client.chat.completions.create(
        model="gpt-4o-mini",
        messages=messages,
        temperature=0.3,  # low for more consistent responses
        max_tokens=500
    )

    return response.choices[0].message.content
```

LLM problems are real and need to be actively managed. Hallucinations happen — the model can invent information confidently. The per-message cost is orders of magnitude higher than classic NLP. Latency is higher. And behavior isn't 100% deterministic, which makes automated testing harder.

Cost at high volume is the biggest brake: 1 million messages per month with GPT-4o can cost several thousand dollars. For smaller volumes and high-value use cases (complex technical support, consultative sales), the ROI justifies it. For basic e-commerce FAQ, it doesn't.

## Decision Matrix by Use Case

| Use Case | Rules | NLP | LLM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static FAQ (10-30 questions) | Ideal | Overkill | Overkill |
| Lead qualification | Ideal | Good | Unnecessary |
| Scheduling with fixed options | Ideal | Good | Unnecessary |
| Technical support with variations | Poor | Good | Excellent |
| Free-form language support | Poor | Fair | Excellent |
| Complex consultative sales | Poor | Poor | Excellent |
| Answering from internal documents | Doesn't work | Fair | Excellent (RAG) |
| High volume (1M+ msgs/month) | Ideal | Good | Expensive |
| Sensitive data (health, legal) | Safe | Safe (Rasa) | Risk |

The central logic is: use rules for predictable flows, NLP for natural language variations in known domains, and LLMs for open conversations where response quality is critical and cost is justifiable.

A hybrid approach often makes more sense: the first level uses rules for the most common flows (representing 80% of volume), and the fallback for everything outside those flows uses an LLM. This controls cost while maintaining quality where it matters.

## Conclusion

There's no ideal chatbot — there's the chatbot appropriate for the context. The decision between rules, NLP, and LLM is an engineering decision that must consider message volume, question complexity, data sensitivity, budget, and error tolerance.

The most expensive mistake is choosing an architecture more complex than necessary — implementing an LLM to answer "what are your business hours" is wasteful. The second most expensive mistake is choosing too simple an architecture for a complex problem — putting a rule-based chatbot on SaaS technical support will frustrate customers without solving anything.
