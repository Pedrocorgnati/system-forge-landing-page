## Direct answer: what WCAG 2.2 compliance means for your business

WCAG 2.2 (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, version 2.2, published October 2023) is the technical standard most regulators reference. Three conformance levels: A (minimum), AA (the practical legal standard worldwide), AAA (aspirational, almost no full sites achieve it).

For a typical business in 2026, "compliant" means Level AA across these four principles:

- **Perceivable** — text alternatives for images, captions for video, sufficient color contrast (4.5:1 for normal text), content reflows on mobile without horizontal scrolling
- **Operable** — every function works with keyboard alone, no flashing content above seizure thresholds, focus indicators visible, no keyboard traps
- **Understandable** — language declared, predictable navigation, form errors clearly labeled and recoverable
- **Robust** — clean HTML semantics, ARIA used correctly, works with major screen readers (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver)

WCAG 2.2 added 9 new criteria over 2.1, mostly focused on cognitive accessibility (no dragging-only interactions, accessible authentication, redundant entry rules). Most SMBs were close on 2.1 but trip on the new 2.2 criteria, especially around drag-and-drop and CAPTCHA.

US litigation is the most active risk for SMBs. ADA "drive-by" lawsuits — plaintiffs visit hundreds of sites, file suits in batches — are a real industry. Most settle quickly because the legal cost of fighting exceeds the settlement. The fastest defense is documented, ongoing accessibility work — a recent VPAT (Voluntary Product Accessibility Template) and an audit trail.

## Accessibility audit: what it costs and what it checks

A real WCAG 2.2 audit is not a Lighthouse score. Lighthouse catches roughly 30% of accessibility issues — useful for monitoring, useless as proof of compliance.

A professional audit includes:

1. **Automated scan** — axe-core, WAVE, Pa11y across every template
2. **Manual keyboard test** — every interactive element reached and operated without a mouse
3. **Screen reader test** — NVDA + JAWS on Windows, VoiceOver on Mac/iOS, TalkBack on Android
4. **Cognitive walkthrough** — forms, error states, multi-step flows
5. **Color contrast verification** — every text+background pair, including hover and focus states
6. **Mobile and zoom test** — 200% zoom, 320px viewport, orientation changes
7. **Documented report** — every issue, mapped to WCAG criterion, severity, remediation guidance

Cost ranges for SMB sites:

- Single-page marketing site: $1,500–$3,000
- Standard 10–30 page business site: $3,000–$5,500
- E-commerce with checkout flow: $4,500–$8,000
- Web app with authenticated areas: $6,000–$15,000

Beware of $300 "audits" — they're automated scans relabeled and won't hold up if a real complaint reaches a court or enforcement agency.

## Making your website accessible: priority fixes

If you're starting from scratch, attack in this order — biggest legal risk and biggest user impact first:

1. **Alt text for every meaningful image** — empty `alt=""` for purely decorative images, real descriptions for content images. Roughly 15% of all WCAG complaints involve missing alt text
2. **Color contrast** — run every text/background combination through a contrast checker. Aim for 4.5:1 minimum, 7:1 for AAA. Cheap fix, huge accessibility lift
3. **Keyboard navigation** — Tab through your entire site without touching the mouse. Every interactive element must be reachable, focusable, and operable. No keyboard traps
4. **Form labels and errors** — every input has a `<label>`, every error message is announced, every field has a clear error recovery path
5. **Heading structure** — one `<h1>` per page, logical `<h2>`/`<h3>` hierarchy, no skipping levels for visual styling
6. **Link text** — no "click here" or "read more." Link text must describe the destination
7. **Video captions and transcripts** — auto-captions are a starting point, not compliance. Manually correct them
8. **Focus indicators** — visible focus ring on every focusable element. Don't disable browser default unless you replace it with something more visible
9. **Page language declared** — `<html lang="en">` is one line and prevents screen reader mispronunciation
10. **Accessible authentication** — WCAG 2.2 added this. CAPTCHA must have an alternative; users shouldn't need to remember/re-enter info

## Accessibility and SEO: the unexpected bonus

Search engines and screen readers consume websites in remarkably similar ways. Both depend on:

- Clean semantic HTML (`<header>`, `<nav>`, `<main>`, `<article>`)

- Mobile-friendly responsive layouts

## Most common mistakes

1. **Trusting the Lighthouse score** — a Lighthouse 100 site can fail 60+ WCAG criteria. Lighthouse is a smoke detector, not a fire marshal
2. **Adding an "accessibility overlay" widget** — these AI-powered overlays (UserWay, AccessiBe, etc.) have been named in over 800 ADA lawsuits in the US. Multiple court rulings explicitly rejected overlays as a defense. They often make sites less accessible, not more
3. **Treating accessibility as a one-time project** — every deploy can introduce regressions. Without automated checks in CI and quarterly manual review, you'll be back where you started in 18 months
4. **Skipping the accessibility statement** — every compliant site needs a public accessibility statement with a feedback channel and contact for complaints. This is required in the EU and a major risk reducer in the US
5. **Letting designers ship inaccessible designs** — color contrast, focus states, alternative text strategy must be in the design system, not patched in code at the end. Train the design team or fight remediation forever

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is my small business actually subject to ADA web requirements?**
Almost certainly yes if you sell to the public, take orders online, or serve as a public accommodation. Multiple federal court rulings (Robles v. Domino's, etc.) have held that the ADA applies to commercial websites, and state laws (CA, NY, CO) are even broader.

**Can I use an accessibility overlay to be compliant?**
No. Overlays have been named as defendants in hundreds of US lawsuits, courts have rejected them as compliance evidence, and the WCAG community uniformly recommends against them. Real remediation is the only durable answer.

**How often should I re-audit?**
Annually at minimum, plus after any major redesign or platform migration. Add automated axe-core checks to your CI pipeline for ongoing monitoring between audits.

**What's the difference between WCAG 2.1 and 2.2?**
2.2 added 9 new success criteria, mostly around cognitive accessibility, drag-and-drop alternatives, focus appearance, accessible authentication, and redundant entry. Sites compliant with 2.1 AA need targeted updates to reach 2.2 AA — usually 5–15 hours of work.

**Does accessibility apply to my mobile app?**
Yes — same WCAG criteria, plus platform-specific guidance (Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Android Accessibility). Mobile audits cost roughly the same as web audits.

**What's the cheapest first step?**
Run WAVE and Lighthouse against your top 5 pages, fix the obvious issues (missing alt, low contrast, missing labels), then commission a real audit. The DIY pass typically eliminates 30–40% of issues for free, making the professional audit cheaper.
