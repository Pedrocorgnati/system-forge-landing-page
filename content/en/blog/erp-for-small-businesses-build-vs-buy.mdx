The decision to use a ready-made ERP or build a custom system is one of the most recurring dilemmas for small and medium business owners in growth mode. The generic ERP saves money today and costs dearly in the future — not in subscription fees, but in lost productivity, parallel spreadsheets, and processes that never quite fit the company's workflow.

This doesn't mean off-the-shelf systems are bad. For most SMBs below a certain level of operational maturity, purchasing a market solution is the right decision. The problem is when the company keeps using the tool out of inertia even after the system has started working against business growth.

This article presents an objective analysis of when each path makes sense, how to assess the transition moment, and what to include in the minimum scope of a custom ERP.

## Ready-Made ERPs for SMBs: QuickBooks, NetSuite, Odoo, and Acumatica

The market has mature solutions for different SMB profiles. Understanding each platform's positioning helps choose the right tool for the company's current stage.

| Platform | Main Focus | Strong Point | Limitation |
|------------|------------|-------------|-----------|
| **QuickBooks** | Accounting + basic management | Simplicity, US market standard | Limited for complex operations |
| **NetSuite** | Full cloud ERP | Scalability, multi-entity | High implementation cost |
| **Odoo** | Modular open source ERP | Flexibility, lower cost | Requires technical skills for customization |
| **Acumatica** | Cloud ERP with industry editions | Flexible licensing | Smaller partner ecosystem |
| **Sage Intacct** | Financial management | Strong reporting | Focused on finance, less operational |

QuickBooks is currently the most common entry point for US SMBs — straightforward, widely supported, and integrates with most business tools. NetSuite is the natural step up when QuickBooks limitations start hurting. Odoo is the choice for businesses wanting modular flexibility on a budget.

Monthly costs for these platforms range from $30 to $2,000+ depending on the plan and operation volume. For a company with $500K/year in revenue, that represents less than 0.5% of revenue — financially justifiable as long as the system delivers value.

## When the Off-the-Shelf ERP No Longer Works

The clearest signal that the company has outgrown the generic ERP is the proliferation of parallel spreadsheets. When employees need an Excel sheet to supplement what the ERP doesn't do, or when the sales team has their own "real" database that gets synced to the official system later, the ERP is failing.

Other warning signs:

**Core processes not supported**: if the business's main process doesn't exist in the ERP — project tracking for contractors, recipe management for compounding pharmacies, service orders for repair shops — the company is working around the system, not with it.

**Impossible or expensive integrations**: when the operation requires integration with industry-specific systems (industrial ERPs, specialized logistics platforms) and the generic platform has no available connector or charges prohibitive API fees.

**Multiple third-party modules**: when the company subscribes to 4-5 different systems that should talk to each other — ERP for accounting, separate CRM, helpdesk system, project management tool — and spends hours weekly doing manual synchronization between them.

**Team growth with efficiency loss**: if doubling the number of employees also doubled manual operational effort, the system isn't scaling with the business.

## Custom ERP: What to Include in the Minimum Scope

A custom ERP doesn't need to do everything on launch day. The most common mistake is trying to migrate 100% of the legacy system's functionality all at once, resulting in 18-month projects that are obsolete before going live.

The minimum viable scope of a custom ERP for SMBs includes:

**Core financial module**:
- Accounts payable and receivable with due dates and alerts
- Cash flow (realized and projected)
- Bank reconciliation
- Cost centers and configurable categories

**Operational module** (varies by sector):
- Main business entity (order, service ticket, project, contract)
- Lifecycle with statuses and owners
- Attachments and interaction history

**Basic tax/accounting module**:
- Invoice generation (standard and electronic where required)
- Tax calculation by product/service type
- Export for accounting integration

**Authentication and roles**:
- Login with MFA
- Access profiles (admin, finance, operations, view-only)
- Action log for audit

What to leave for future versions: advanced BI, marketplace integrations, full HR module, mobile app. Features that seem essential before launch frequently have low usage in the first months.

## ERP Migration: How Not to Lose Historical Data

Historical data migration is where ERP projects typically fail silently. The company goes live with the new system, the history "will be migrated later" — and it never is. Two years later, no one can answer simple questions like "what was that client's revenue in 2022?"

The migration strategy must be treated as a mandatory deliverable, not a subsequent task:

**1. Source data audit**: before migrating, understand the real state of data in the legacy system. Null values that shouldn't be null, duplicate entities, broken relationships. The cleanup happens at the source before migration.

**2. Schema mapping**: formal document mapping each field from the old system to the corresponding field in the new one. Fields without exact correspondence need an explicit decision.

**3. Layered migration**: migrate master data first (customers, suppliers, products), then historical transactional data (closed orders, financial entries), finally open data (active orders, pending receivables).

**4. Parallel operation period**: keep both systems running simultaneously for 30-60 days, with daily reconciliation of financial balances. Expensive, but essential for businesses with critical operations.

**5. Legacy archiving**: the old system should not be shut down at go-live. Keep it in read-only mode for at least 12 months for historical queries.

## Conclusion

The build vs buy ERP decision isn't a matter of company size — it's a matter of operational fit. A $20M-revenue company may work very well with QuickBooks if its processes are standard. A $2M company may need a custom system if its business model is sufficiently unique.

The reliable signal that it's time to build custom is when the sum of the cost of adapting processes to the generic ERP (lost time, rework, parallel spreadsheets) exceeds the investment in a proprietary system.
