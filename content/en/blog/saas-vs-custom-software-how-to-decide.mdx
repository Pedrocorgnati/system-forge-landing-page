Every growing company eventually has to answer this question: buy a ready-made SaaS or build something custom? The wrong answer can mean years of dependency on a platform that doesn't scale with the business, or months of development wasted on something that already existed perfectly in the market.

There's no universal answer. But there is a clear analysis process that separates well-founded decisions from blind bets.

## When the Ready-Made SaaS Is the Right Answer

The simplest rule: if the functionality you need **is not a competitive differentiator**, don't build — buy.

Email marketing, user authentication, payment processing, e-invoicing, video conferencing, document signing — none of these processes differentiate your company in the market. They're operational commodities. Building them custom is a waste of engineering energy that could go toward what actually differentiates your product or service.

Other signals that the ready-made SaaS is the right path:

- **The problem is well-defined and generic.** CRM, SMB ERP, helpdesk, HR management — there are dozens of mature solutions, with years of continuous improvement, that solve these problems better than any small team could in 6 months.
- **The technical team is lean.** Every proprietary system needs maintenance, updating, monitoring. A custom system maintained by a two-developer team is a liability.
- **The need is immediate.** A SaaS with a monthly plan is available in hours. A custom system takes 3 to 12 months to reach the maturity level you need.

## When Custom Software Pays Off

Custom software makes sense when the process you want to automate **is the business itself** — or when market solutions create more friction than value.

Examples of when building is worthwhile:

- **The process is proprietary and hard to generalize.** A unique pricing methodology, an order routing logic specific to your industry, a risk analysis model you've developed over years — this doesn't exist in any SaaS because it's your differentiator.
- **Integration with legacy systems is too complex.** In industries like logistics, manufacturing, and retail, old ERP systems have proprietary or nonexistent APIs. Sometimes it's cheaper to build than to integrate.
- **Volume justifies it economically.** A SaaS at $5,000/month over 5 years costs $300,000. If a custom system can be built for $150,000 and maintained for $20,000/year, the break-even happens in year three.
- **Regulatory or security requirements are restrictive.** Financial sector, healthcare, government — there are situations where data simply cannot leave company infrastructure.

## Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) in Practice

The most honest comparison between SaaS and custom software runs through total cost of ownership over a 3-5 year horizon. The most common mistake is comparing development cost to subscription cost — and forgetting the hidden costs on both sides.

| Cost item | SaaS | Custom software |
|-----------|------|----------------|
| Initial implementation | Low (onboarding) | High (development) |
| Team training | Medium | High (new system) |
| Customizations | Limited or expensive | Unlimited |
| Annual maintenance | Included in subscription | 15-20% of development cost |
| Upgrades and new features | Automatic | On demand (additional cost) |
| Price increase risk | High | None |
| Discontinuation risk | Present | None |
| Integration with other systems | Via API (when available) | Built custom |

A practical estimate for a mid-sized system:

```
Scenario A — Ready-made SaaS
Monthly subscription:         $4,000/month
Cost over 5 years:            $240,000
Billed customizations:        $40,000
5-year TCO:                   $280,000

Scenario B — Custom software
Development:                  $120,000
Infrastructure (monthly):     $700/month
Annual maintenance:           $18,000/year
5-year TCO:                   $252,000
```

The numbers converge — and the custom software advantage grows as the business scales and usage volume increases without additional cost.

## Lock-in: The Forgotten SaaS Risk

Lock-in is the cost that doesn't appear in the TCO spreadsheet, but often decides the discussion. When a company depends on a SaaS for years, it accumulates data, configurations, integrations, and internal processes built around that platform. Migrating becomes progressively more difficult and expensive.

The most common types of lock-in:

**Data lock-in:** The SaaS stores years of history in a proprietary format. The export is incomplete, requires manual processing, or simply doesn't exist for certain data types.

**Integration lock-in:** Dozens of internal systems connect via the SaaS API. A migration requires rewriting all those integrations.

**Process lock-in:** The entire team works with the SaaS vocabulary and workflows. Changing implies retraining and productivity loss.

**Price lock-in:** Over time, the vendor knows the migration cost is high — and uses this to raise prices above market. 20-40% annual increases are not uncommon in established platforms.

To mitigate lock-in without giving up the SaaS: require data export in open format in the contract, document all integrations, and define a contingency plan before becoming critically dependent on any third-party platform.

## Conclusion

The SaaS vs custom software decision is rarely binary. Mature companies use both approaches in balance: SaaS for everything that's a commodity, proprietary systems for what's a differentiator.

What distinguishes founders and CTOs who make good decisions in this area is clarity about what truly differentiates the business — and the discipline not to spend engineering energy solving problems already solved in the market.
